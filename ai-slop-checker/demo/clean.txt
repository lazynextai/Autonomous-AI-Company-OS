The bakery opens at six. By five forty-five the oven room smells of yeast and
burnt sugar, and the first tray of sourdough comes out at six ten with a crust
that cracks when you touch it. Maya sets the loaves on the counter in order of
size, largest first, because the morning customers buy the big round one
before they look at anything else.

Orders arrive by phone and by foot. The woman in the blue coat asks for a
seeded loaf and a currant bun, pays with coins, and is gone before the kettle
boils. A child presses his nose to the glass and points at the chocolate
croissant, which Maya wraps in paper without saying a word.

At noon the tables are wiped, the floor is swept, and the day's receipts are
counted twice. The flour dust settles on the window ledge. Maya locks the door
at two, walks to the market for lemons, and starts the dough for tomorrow
evening while the radio plays a jazz station she does not recognize.
